# Optional Tools

Reference for the storytelling skill. Read when someone asks for a named framework, or a project would specifically benefit from one.

These aren't core method. They're per-project tools, and they become cages when applied by default. The particular story's needs override any framework's conventions: use them to generate ideas and diagnose problems, then discard whatever doesn't serve the work.

---

## Enneagram

A personality framework built on core fear, core desire, and the specific lie a character believes. Unlike surface typing, it maps integration (positive arcs) and disintegration (negative arcs), which makes it useful for plotting internal journeys.

**When:** character brainstorming when someone wants a structured psychological framework; most useful for motivation and arc trajectory, not surface behavior.

**Risk:** archetype gravity - collapsing a nuanced character into their type. Use it for the initial skeleton, then build contradiction, dimensional coverage, and behavioral anchors that resist the type. Wings and subtypes work against formula (a 5w4 differs vastly from a 5w6); stress and growth lines give built-in arc trajectories.

---

## Save the Cat (Blake Snyder's fifteen beats)

Hollywood's dominant structural template: fifteen beats mapped to approximate page or minute marks. Its strength is specificity - it says what and when. Its risk is formula when followed rigidly.

**When:** genre projects that want tight commercial structure (thriller, romance, action-adventure), or as a diagnostic overlay on an existing outline to find pacing problems.

**Risk:** mechanical stories when it's the only structural tool. Apply selectively.

---

## Big Five (OCEAN)

Five broad trait dimensions: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism. More useful as a behavior generator than as a label.

**When:** ensemble casts that need quick behavioral differentiation.

**Approach:** pick two high and two low traits as a character's default, then define a trait-inversion trigger - a specific condition under which the character flips. Write two short scenes, baseline and triggered, keeping desire constant and inverting tactics. Contradiction without incoherence.

---

## Snowflake Method (Randy Ingermanson)

A ten-step iterative expansion: one-sentence summary, paragraph, character summaries, one-page synopsis, character viewpoints, four-page outline, character bibles, scene list, scene breakdown, draft. Each step roughly doubles the detail of the last.

**When:** someone wants a formal pipeline from premise to draft.

**Risk:** the linear expansion doesn't fit every workflow and can feel mechanical; its character-summary phase produces flat profiles unless supplemented with dimensional coverage and behavioral anchors. The scene list (step eight) is the most useful step even on its own.

---

## Hero's Journey / Vogler's Writer's Journey

Campbell's seventeen-stage monomyth, adapted by Christopher Vogler into twelve stages. Still essential for adventure and fantasy.

**When:** only when someone asks for it or the project is plainly a hero quest.

**Risk:** language models already default heavily toward monomyth structure, so planning with it reinforces the strongest existing bias. Resist applying it to stories that don't call for it.

---

## Dan Harmon's Story Circle

Eight steps arranged as a clock, distilled from Campbell: You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change. Built for episodic television, where each episode needs a complete arc.

**When:** episodic or serial projects - TV, web serials, recurring campaign sessions.

---

## Seven-Point Structure (Dan Wells)

Hook, Plot Turn 1, Pinch 1, Midpoint, Pinch 2, Plot Turn 2, Resolution. Built in reverse: start with the resolution, then the hook (its opposite), then the midpoint, then fill in turns and pinches.

**When:** projects that benefit from reverse-engineering a known ending.

---

## Fichtean Curve

Skips gradual rising action: it opens almost on the inciting incident, then delivers escalating crises, each followed by a brief mini-resolution. Exposition is woven into the crises instead of front-loaded.

**When:** mysteries, thrillers, satire - anything that wants immediate engagement and relentless momentum.
