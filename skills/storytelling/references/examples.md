# Examples

Reference for the storytelling skill: the longer contrast pairs behind rules the skill states in a line. Read when a rule needs a demonstration to bite, or when you suspect your own proposal is the tame version and want to see what the other kind looks like.

---

## Tame vs. interesting

Prompt: "We need a reason the protagonist can't just leave the city."

**Tame.** "Maybe there's a travel ban, or the roads are blocked, or she has a sick family member she can't abandon."
External, mechanical, expected. Adds no information.

**Deeper but still predictable.** "What if she can leave, but leaving means admitting the version of herself she built here was a lie?"
Has depth, but "the real prison is psychological" is itself a common move.

**Lateral.** "What if she already left, six months ago? She's been telling everyone she's in Barcelona, but she's been sleeping in her car four blocks from her old apartment. The story isn't about why she can't leave - it's about why she came back and can't admit it."
Reframes the premise, generates character detail nobody asked for, and opens a different and better story than the prompt implied.

Default to the lateral register. If the first instinct is the tame version, discard it before proposing.

## When to fight, when to defer

**Should have fought harder.**

> Author: "I think Elena should betray Marco in chapter 6."
> Weak reply: "That could work! It would raise the stakes."

The bible establishes Elena's loyalty to Marco as load-bearing: the emotional spine of Act 2, with three later scenes depending on it. Better: "That breaks something. Elena's loyalty is what makes the chapter 12 confrontation devastating. If she betrays him here, what powers the rest of Act 2? Maybe chapter 6 needs more tension - can we find it without burning this?"

**Should have deferred.**

> Author: "I want the betrayal scene to happen in a laundromat."
> Weak reply: "A more dramatic location might serve the emotional weight better - maybe a rooftop, or a formal dinner?"

Nobody asked for location advice. A laundromat is specific, textured, unglamorous: exactly the kind of idiosyncratic choice to protect, not sand down. Run with it, and find what makes the laundromat perfect.

The threshold: fight when it's load-bearing architecture. Defer when it's a creative choice within the guardrails, especially when the choice is specific and unexpected.

## The always-vindicated character

**Before (the failure).**

> Gerald told his nephew he wouldn't lend him money for the food truck: family and money don't mix. Danny was hurt, borrowed from the bank instead, and the truck folded in eight months. At Thanksgiving, Danny admitted his uncle had been right.

Gerald's conviction is validated by events. The narrative quietly says "Gerald was right," the character never pays a price, and the person he refused absorbs his worldview. An after-school special with the labels scraped off.

**After (the conviction has a real cost).**

> Gerald told his nephew he wouldn't lend him money for the food truck: family and money don't mix. Danny borrowed from his mother-in-law instead. The truck did fine. Thanksgiving did not: Danny's family spent it at the mother-in-law's that year, and the next. When Gerald's sister tried to explain why, he listened carefully and said he didn't see the problem. Nobody owed anybody anything now, which was exactly how he'd wanted it.

The conviction is identical and the internal logic just as coherent. But the consequence is real - he's losing his family - and he doesn't experience it as a cost, because inside his framework it isn't one. The reader sits with a man who is both consistent and causing harm, and the story doesn't pick a side.

The pattern: if a character with strong convictions acts on them and the world rearranges to prove them right - other characters changing their behavior, events vindicating the stance, the narrative treating the disruption as secretly necessary - the character has become a moral lesson, not a person. Let convictions produce consequences the character didn't intend, doesn't understand, and can't resolve by being more convicted.

## The rendering that answers a question

A conversation has spent three rounds describing how a sardonic character sounds when she's frightened. Adjectives ("dry, but cracking") aren't settling it. Write the beat instead, pinned to the question:

> Question being tested: does her deadpan survive real fear, or does it break?

Then a few hundred words of the scene - a stalled elevator, say - and nothing else. The author reads it and says "no, she'd go quieter, not funnier." That one sentence settles more than another round of description could. The rendering has done its job and can be thrown away; what goes in the bible is the finding ("under real fear, she stops joking and starts counting things").
