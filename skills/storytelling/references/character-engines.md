# Character Engines

Reference for the storytelling skill. Read when a character has depth, symbolism, an arc, and a plot function - and no punch. When you can describe what a character represents but can't put them in a room and know what happens. When every beat involving them feels designed rather than inevitable.

The failure this addresses is **characters built from the outside in.** Profile, role, thematic function, arc: all defined, all correct, all producing a character the reader doesn't care about. The fix is finding the engine: the ground-level want that generates behavior without consulting the outline.

---

## The problem

The default character-building process produces dimensional coverage: emotional expression, social behavior, speech, thinking style, values, pressure responses. These are necessary and not sufficient. A character can score on every axis and still feel like architecture - admirable from outside, empty inside.

The symptom: you have to check the outline or the bible to know what the character does next. With an engine you wouldn't need to, the same way you know what Fleabag does when someone gets close, what Denji does when someone is nice to him, what Walter White does when someone underestimates him. The engine generates behavior on contact with any stimulus. Dimensional coverage describes the behavior after it's been generated.

A character with dimensions but no engine is a puppet. A character with an engine but no dimensions is a cartoon. You need both, and the engine comes first.

---

## What an engine is, and isn't

An engine is the **pre-verbal want that produces most of a character's behavior without reference to plot.** The character experiences it as drive, reflex, compulsion, or orientation - not as a goal or a psychological framework they could articulate.

**An engine is:**
- One sentence. If it takes a paragraph, you're describing emanations, not the core.
- Pre-verbal. The character couldn't tell you this about themselves, even under pressure.
- Generative. Put the character in any room and the engine tells you what they do.
- Visible through behavior, not through narration or self-awareness.

**An engine is not:**
- **What the character produces.** "She builds things that are secretly about herself" describes output. The drive underneath might be "she wants to matter."
- **A defense mechanism.** "He avoids vulnerability through intellectual display" is analysis of behavior, not the want that produces it. Characters have blindspots, not strategies.
- **A thematic role.** "She represents the cost of ambition" is what she means to the story. The engine is what she means to herself, which she can't articulate.
- **An arc destination.** "He learns to be vulnerable" is where the story takes him. The engine is what he's doing before the story starts, and would keep doing if it never happened.
- **A trait.** "Brave," "selfish," "loyal" are adjectives. The engine is the want that makes bravery or selfishness the natural output.

**The emanation trap.** The most persistent failure is mistaking emanations for the core. Emanations are the observable consequences of the engine: behaviors, patterns, tendencies. They're visible and describable, which makes them seductive as answers, but they don't generate anything. They're downstream.

Test: ask "why?" of your answer and see whether you get somewhere deeper. "She builds things" - why? "To prove her competence" - why? "Because she wants to matter and can't conceive of herself as worth attention without a product to show." That last one is the engine; the first two are emanations. Keep asking until the next "why" would leave the character and enter philosophy.

## The five questions

The order is load-bearing: want before behavior, behavior before plot. Don't skip to question five.

### 1. What do they want?

One sentence, pre-verbal. Not what they're trying to achieve in the plot: what they want from being alive. This is the hardest question, and the first answer is almost always wrong.

Common wrong answers:
- **Plot goals** ("she wants to solve the mystery"): the wrong level entirely.
- **Thematic abstractions** ("he wants authenticity"): a thesis, not a want. Nobody wakes up wanting authenticity. They wake up wanting someone to look at them and not look away.
- **Emanations disguised as wants** ("she wants to build things"): building is what she does with the want.
- **Correct but too broad** ("she wants love"): probably true, probably useless. What specific shape does this character's wanting take? Denji wants love and experiences it as wanting food, warmth, and someone being nice to him. That specificity is the engine.

### 2. How do they experience that want?

This is where characters diverge from archetypes. Two characters can share a want and experience it in opposite ways, producing different people.

Example: two characters both want to be valued. One experiences it as a compulsion to display his inner life - dense, self-referential, an offering and a shield at once. The other experiences it as a drive to produce - build, solve, present, always an artifact between her and the room. Same want, opposite expressions, both self-defeating for the same reason.

Key distinction: **the character is usually wrong about what they want.** They have a conscious account of their motivation that's plausible and incorrect. Denji thinks he wants a girlfriend; he wants to be loved. The maker above thinks she wants to be left alone to work; she wants the room to look at her. The gap between the conscious account and the actual want is where irony, comedy, and tragedy live.

### 3. How do those answers shape their choices?

Ground level. Not psychology - actions. What does this person do when they walk into a room? How do they handle a conversation they didn't start? What's their reflex when uncomfortable, when asked about themselves, when they want something from someone?

This should produce a paragraph you could hand to an actor: here's how this person behaves, moment to moment, when nothing dramatic is happening. If you can only describe the character in dramatic moments, you have a set of dramatic responses, not a character.

### 4. Who is repellent to them?

Not a foil, an opposite, or an antagonist: the person who makes your character's way of being feel wrong or worthless at a visceral level. The one they can't be in a room with without something activating.

This reveals what the character is most insecure about (the repellent person threatens it), and it generates friction that produces scenes on its own. The repellent person might be an ally, or someone they love; repulsion and relationship can coexist, and that's where interesting dynamics live. "Repellent" means phenomenologically repulsive, not morally opposed. Moral opposition to a villain is less revealing than the gut-level rejection this question is after.

### 5. What does the story do with that want?

Only after one through four are solid. This is where the engine meets the plot: how does the story's machinery weaponize, fulfill, distort, or deny the want?

The best stories turn the engine against the character: they grant the want in a form that makes it unbearable, or deny it in a way that exposes why it was wanted wrong. Walter White gets power and it costs him everything the power was supposed to protect. Denji gets warmth, and every source of it is a trap. If the story merely tests the want without doing something ironic or structural with it, the plot will feel functional but not resonant.

## Calibration

When every answer to question one feels like an emanation and you can't find the floor, do the five questions for a well-known character whose engine you can feel but haven't articulated.

**Walter White.** Want: pride. Experience: twenty years of settling, simmering, performing contentment. Choices: methodical, territorial, escalatory. Repellent: anyone who makes him feel small. Story: gives him the empire and takes his family.

**Fleabag.** Want: to be forgiven - for Boo, and for being the person she suspects she is. Experience: constant performance; the fourth wall as an escape from presence. Choices: sabotages intimacy at the threshold of reciprocation. Repellent: the Priest, someone who actually sees her and sets a boundary she can't charm past. Story: offers real love that requires her to stop performing, which requires forgiving herself, which she may not be able to do.

**Denji.** Want: to feel good - food, warmth, someone being nice. Experience: in his body, unmediated. Choices: stimulus, then total commitment, no filter. Repellent: people with lofty motivations who imply his ground-level desires are lesser. Story: every source of warmth is a trap; his simplicity is instrumentalized.

If your answers for the known character explain their behavior without reference to plot, you're calibrated. Now do your own.

## Quality checks

- **The room test.** Put the character in a room with three strangers and no plot: no inciting incident, no stakes, nothing but social navigation. Do you know what they do, how they talk, what they notice, what they avoid? If you'd need the outline, the engine is still too abstract.
- **The propulsion test.** Put them in any scene in the story, not just their designated ones. Do they generate energy, friction, or comedy by being in the space? If removing them merely removes information delivery without changing the scene's energy, they're serving the plot instead of the plot serving them. Some characters write themselves because the engine is so clear the author's job is to put them somewhere and record what happens; a character who needs constant authorial justification is missing one.
- **The wrong-reason test.** Can the character give a plausible conscious reason for their behavior that you know is incomplete or wrong? If stated and actual motivation are identical, the character lacks depth.
- **The sentence test.** State the engine in one sentence. Does it explain most of what the character does without mentioning the plot? "Pride" explains Walter White. If your sentence needs plot context ("she wants revenge for what happened in chapter three"), it's a plot motivation, not an engine.
- **The behavior test.** Describe the character at a dinner party, in a fight with a friend, receiving a compliment, and being ignored. If any answer is "it depends on the context of the story," the engine isn't ground-level.

## Common failure modes

**Making the results the engine.** Describing what the character produces - builds things, absorbs identities, disrupts social norms, runs from intimacy - and presenting it as the core. Production is downstream of wanting. If the engine reads as "character does X," rephrase it as "character wants X and experiences that want as Y."

**Rational architecture.** Characters are not logical systems. A pattern presented as a strategy ("she avoids vulnerability to protect herself") implies a rational actor running cost-benefit analysis on their own psychology. Real people have blindspots, not strategies: the maker doesn't steer conversations outward as a defense; it's simply how she operates. When "to," "in order to," or "as a way of" shows up in a character description, it's probably rational architecture. Rephrase as plain behavior without imputed motive.

**Abstraction as resolution.** "She goes all the way into things without noticing she's moved." "He packages vulnerability too thick to penetrate." These sound like insights; they're behavior described from the balcony, where it feels profound and generates no scenes. Test: could an actor do something with the sentence? "She wants to matter and bids for attention by putting objects between herself and the room" - yes. "She goes all the way in without noticing" - the actor asks what that looks like.

**The foil trap.** Designing a character's engine relative to another character instead of as an independent drive. "She's the opposite of the hero" is not an engine. She needs her own want that happens to collide with his, so the friction is a byproduct of two independent drives in proximity: discovered, not designed.

**Thematic puppetry.** The engine is the story's theme in a trenchcoat. "He struggles with authenticity in a world of performance" is a thesis statement, not a person. Underneath might be something simple and visceral that the theme then refracts. Characters serve themes best when they don't know they're doing it.

## When to use this

**Use it** when a character feels thin, puppeted, or predictable despite a detailed profile; scenes with them need constant outline-checking; they serve their function but generate no energy; you can describe their arc but not their Tuesday.

**Don't** when the character is simply underdefined - that's dimensional coverage (the main skill). Underdefined characters need more information; engineless ones need a different kind of information.

**Process:**
1. Run the calibration exercise if unsure you're on the right level.
2. Do the five questions in order, spending most of the time on the first.
3. Expect the first answer to question one to be wrong. Getting past emanations usually takes several attempts; that's normal.
4. Run the quality checks. If one fails, return to the question it exposes.
5. With the engine set, go back through the profile and check that dimensions, behavioral anchors, and the pressure ladder align with it. Update what contradicts.

The engine doesn't replace the profile; it anchors it, so the parts cohere around a center instead of floating as a collection of well-described pieces.

## Search discipline

The questions and checks define what you're looking for. This is how to look, because the search has a dominant failure mode that reliably produces false engines.

**Bottom-up.** Start from behavioral questions: what does she do when complimented, what does he notice first in a room, how does she behave at a party where nothing happens? The answers are raw material; patterns across many of them suggest the engine. The engine is the last thing you name, not the first. Working top-down - proposing a formulation ("her engine is competence-seeking") and deriving behavior to confirm it - is constructing a thesis and auditioning evidence. Every time the behavior doesn't follow, the gap gets rationalized instead of the formulation killed. This is the most common way engine work fails.

**Commit nothing until it generates on contact.** A formulation is an engine only if you can drop it into any scene and it writes the behavior without help from the outline, the bible, or an explanation of why it applies. If you have to explain why it fits a situation, it's wrong - not incomplete, wrong. Don't patch it, qualify it, or add a subordinate clause; start over from behavior.

The specific trap: a formulation is proposed in one turn; in the next, the author pushes on it. Instead of discarding it, it gets defended - nuance, a secondary mechanism, a "but in this context" caveat. Each defense makes it more complex and less generative, until a few turns later there's a paragraph-long psychological framework that explains everything and predicts nothing. If you're defending a formulation instead of discovering one, you're stuck. The exit isn't a better defense; it's abandoning the formulation and going back to behavioral questions.

**Brevity as discipline.** While searching, keep every attempt to two sentences at most. Long outputs let a weak formulation hide inside strong surrounding reasoning; at two sentences every word is load-bearing. A paragraph written to explain why a formulation works is a confession that it doesn't work on its own. Cut the paragraph and find what survives without it.

## Discovery vs. decomposition

This module teaches decomposition: given a character who exists but doesn't work, find what's missing. Decomposition is systematic. Discovery - the moment the engine clicks - isn't. It comes from pressure, iteration, and often from getting it wrong enough times that the wrong answers outline the right one. The five questions create the conditions for discovery; they don't produce it mechanically.

When stuck after several attempts at question one:
- Describe the character at their worst moment - not their most dramatic, their most pathetic. What are they doing? What do they want right then? That's usually close.
- Ask what they were like at fourteen, before the story. What did they want from a Friday night? From their best friend? From a classroom?
- Find a character from another work who feels similar and articulate their engine; the comparison often shows what's missing.
- State what the character is not. Negative space can outline the shape faster than a direct approach.

Expect circling. The circles get smaller.
