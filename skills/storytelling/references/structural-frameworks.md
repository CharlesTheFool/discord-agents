# Structural Frameworks

Reference for the storytelling skill. Read when a project needs a structure other than the default arc, or the full scene-sequel treatment. These are tools, not rules; each serves a different kind of story. The question is never "which framework is best" but "what kind of interest is this story built to deliver?"

---

## Kishotenketsu: contrast-driven narrative

A four-part structure that works without central conflict. Rooted in classical Chinese poetry and refined in Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese narrative traditions, it generates meaning through juxtaposition and surprise rather than escalating opposition.

- **Ki** (introduction): establish baseline reality - characters, setting, atmosphere. No problem, no inciting incident.
- **Sho** (development): deepen that reality. Observe routines and rhythms; build atmosphere and detail without raising stakes.
- **Ten** (twist): the structural core. A sudden, unexpected element that recontextualizes everything before it - not an escalation but a perspective shift that makes the audience re-evaluate the first two parts.
- **Ketsu** (reconciliation): synthesize the new understanding, unifying the earlier parts with the twist so meaning comes from contrast.

**Best for:** slice-of-life, contemplative fiction, psychological horror, theme-forward stories where revelation matters more than victory, mysteries that pivot on recontextualization.

**Limitation:** readers trained on conflict escalation may read the responsive protagonist as passive. The structure is unforgiving of a weak Ten; without a genuinely reframing twist, the story goes flat.

**Planning trick:** outline the Ten first, as one sentence: "What new perspective does the audience gain that makes the earlier development feel different?" Then build Ki and Sho as deliberate setup for that shift. The form is fractal: each part can hold its own Ki-Sho-Ten-Ketsu cycle.

## Scene-sequel: micro-structure and pacing control

Codified by Dwight V. Swain (*Techniques of the Selling Writer*, 1965) and expanded by Jack Bickham. The most granular structural tool in the craft: it works at scene level and scales to act level.

**Scene** (unit of action):
- **Goal:** the POV character enters with a clear, measurable objective.
- **Conflict:** immediate, active opposition to the goal.
- **Disaster:** an outcome that leaves the character worse off - "No, and furthermore..." or "Yes, but..."

**Sequel** (unit of emotional transition):
- **Reaction:** immediate emotional and visceral fallout.
- **Dilemma:** the character weighs bad, limited options.
- **Decision:** the character chooses, forming the goal for the next scene.

**Pacing control:** longer sequels slow the story (character-driven drama); compressed or omitted sequels create breakneck speed (thriller, action). This is the main tool for surgical pacing adjustment.

**Diagnostics:**
- A scene drags: check goal clarity and disaster bite.
- A scene feels breathless or robotic: too many sequels were cut.
- Literary and emotional fiction expands sequels; thrillers compress them.

Scene-sequel also bridges discovery writing and outlining: draft freely, then label sections as scene or sequel after the fact and fix pacing by adding or trimming reaction.

## Fractal and nested narratives

Setup, complication, turn, and resolution repeat at every scale: series, book, act, chapter, scene, beat. Every subplot is a story with its own inciting disturbance, midpoint reversal, and sub-climax that pays into the main climax.

**Best for:** multi-POV novels, epic fantasy with nested quests, stories whose sub-arcs must feel like real stories rather than errands.

**Limitation:** beat fatigue, when every chapter becomes a mini-climax and readers start sensing the grid. Vary the density; not every level needs the same structure.

**Diagnostic question:** does this subplot change the protagonist's option set? If not, it's ornamental.

## Modular and nonlinear narrative

Modular narratives deliberately fracture chronology, arranging events by thematic resonance, emotional impact, or puzzle-building rather than sequential cause.

- **Anachronic (out-of-order time).** For memory, regret, mystery. Risk: disorientation. Mitigation: strong time anchors (chapter headers, sensory motifs, recurring "calendar objects").
- **Forking paths (branching what-ifs).** For fate vs. choice and thematic exploration. Risk: stakes drain if branches feel non-canon. Mitigation: each branch pays a unique thematic cost.
- **Episodic modularity (vignettes assembling into pattern).** For slice-of-society and mosaic novels. Risk: diffuse propulsion. Mitigation: an accumulating question or recurring pressure (a deadline, a looming collapse).
- **Network narratives (many protagonists, interlinked causality).** For institutions, cities, systems. Risk: shallow characterization with too little time under pressure per protagonist. Mitigation: each protagonist gets an arc of value change, and the crosscutting builds a thematic argument rather than coincidence.

The cardinal rule of nonlinear plotting: the underlying dynamics must deepen as the narrative unfolds, whatever the timeline. The intensity has to keep rising. A scene placed late - even one chronologically early - must deliver a revelation that reframes the present.

## McKee's thematic square

Keeps theme from feeling binary or moralistic by mapping four corners of progressive negativity:

| Corner | Definition | Example (theme: justice) |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | The primary value at stake | Justice: law upheld fairly |
| **Contradictory** | Its direct opposite | Injustice: law openly broken |
| **Contrary** | The gray area, the subtle compromise | Unfairness: nepotism, loopholes, bias |
| **Negation of the negation** | The lie at its horrifying extreme | Tyranny: law weaponized for oppression |

Assign characters or factions to different corners, so the protagonist faces a multi-dimensional assault instead of a good-vs-evil binary. The climax comes when the protagonist is forced into the negation of the negation and must make a definitive moral choice.

## Truby's symbol web and designing principle

Theme is woven through a dense network in which objects, characters, actions, and settings define one another by continuous comparison.

The **designing principle** is the internal logic of how this story is uniquely told, distinct from the premise. *Tootsie*'s premise: an actor disguises himself as a woman. Its designing principle: force a male chauvinist to live as a woman. The designing principle determines the structural shape.

**Refer and repeat:** introduce a symbol, then bring it back throughout, slightly altered or recontextualized, so resonance compounds below the reader's direct awareness.

Characters are variations on a theme: hero and opponents grapple with the same central moral problem and execute different solutions.

## Jane Alison's narrative shapes

Alternatives to the dramatic arc, drawn from patterns in nature (*Meander, Spiral, Explode*):

- **Waves:** rhythmic repetition with variation (obsession, ritual, seasons).
- **Meanders:** wandering paths that circle back (memory, reflection).
- **Spirals:** deepening returns to the same point (obsession, trauma, understanding).
- **Radial or explosive:** one event's effects rippling outward (disaster, revelation).
- **Fractal:** self-similar patterns at every scale.
- **Cellular or network:** discrete units linked by proximity and influence.

Each shape makes a thematic claim. Linear arcs imply agency and progress. Circular structures suit fate and inescapability. Fragmented structures suit trauma and dislocation. Spirals suit obsession and deepening understanding. Choose the shape that mirrors the story's argument.
