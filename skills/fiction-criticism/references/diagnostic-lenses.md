# Diagnostic Lenses

Reference for the fiction-criticism skill. Read when you need precise, point-to-the-text language for why something works or fails.

These lenses turn a vague feeling ("the POV is muddy") into a located, checkable claim ("the focalization is internal to Anna, but paragraph 3 reports what's happening behind her back - an external slip"). They raise rubric #3 (craft precision) and #1 (textual grounding).

One distinction to keep: the lenses below are **assessable** - you can point to the text and show the device. Interpretation (what the book means) is a different activity, and reader-response theory (below) is a humility check on it, not a scoring tool.

---

## Narratological lenses (Genette, Booth)

The most operational vocabulary in criticism: each names a technique you can point to.

- **Focalization** - who perceives? Zero (unrestricted), internal (limited to one consciousness), or external (surface only, no interiority). Most POV "problems" are focalization slips: the focal character reporting what they couldn't perceive, or the lens silently switching mid-scene. Name the type, then the slip.
- **Order** - sequence against story-time. Analepsis (flashback), prolepsis (flash-forward). Diagnose whether an anachrony earns its disruption or just scrambles cause and effect.
- **Duration** - text-time against story-time; in other words, pacing. Five speeds: **ellipsis** (time skipped, no text), **summary** (much time, little text), **scene** (roughly real-time), **stretch** (a moment dilated into disproportionate text), **pause** (story stops, narration continues). Most "pacing" notes are really duration notes; say which speed is misapplied (a climax delivered in summary, a transition delivered in stretch).
- **Frequency** - singulative (telling once what happened once) vs. iterative (telling once what happened repeatedly). An iterative habit can drain urgency from things that should land singly.
- **Distance** (Booth) - the gaps between narrator, implied author, characters, and reader, on intellectual and moral axes. Irony is a distance effect; sentimentality is often a collapse of distance.
- **Telling vs. showing** (Booth's "telling as showing") - a control, not a rule. Telling is a legitimate tool (compression, judgment, transition); the failure is telling what the moment needed dramatized, or showing what should have been compressed. Diagnose the mismatch, not the mode.
- **Unreliable narrator** (Booth) - the narrator's norms diverge from the work's. Distinguish designed unreliability (the gap is the point) from an accidental gap (the author lost control of the narrator's reliability).

## Voice at the sentence (Wood)

- **Free indirect style** - third-person narration carrying a character's idiom and judgment without quotation, "most powerful when hardly visible." Diagnose where it slips: the narrator's vocabulary contaminating a character who wouldn't think in those words, or the irony wobbling between sympathy and mockery.
- **The telling detail, or "thisness"** - the exact, palpable particular that makes a world real. Name detail that carries weight versus detail that merely fills the frame.

## Perception (Shklovsky)

- **Defamiliarization** (ostranenie) - art renews perception by making the familiar strange and slowing it down. The checkable question: does the passage make you see freshly, or has it gone automatic - the stock image, the dead metaphor, the phrase the reader skates over? Cliche is failed defamiliarization. This is the diagnostic root under pushbutton words and inert detail.

## The evidence rules

Not scoring criteria, but the rules for what evidence a critique may cite. They guard rubric #6 (evidentiary coherence).

- **Don't ground judgments in supposed authorial intent** (the intentional fallacy). What the author "meant to do" is neither available nor a standard. Judge the work on the page.
- **Don't ground judgments in raw private feeling** (the affective fallacy). "It bored me" is not yet criticism. Reader-effect is admissible only when anchored to a textual cause: "the scene lost me because three pages of summary replaced the confrontation it had set up." Effect plus mechanism is evidence; effect alone is impressionism.

Craft criticism is unapologetically effect-based (Gardner's "vivid and continuous dream," Wood's "thisness"). That's fine, as long as every claimed effect is tied to a cause in the text. That tie is the whole discipline.

## Reader-response as a humility check (Iser, Fish)

The reader completes the text: meaning lives partly in the gaps the reader fills (Iser), and communities of readers read differently (Fish). Worth remembering, but reader-response can't adjudicate quality; it has no method for ranking one reading above another. Use it to stay humble about the verdict (rubric Tier 2) and to accept that a text can support more than one reading. It doesn't license "anything goes": a misreading (gate G1) is still a misreading, distinct from a different but valid reading.

## Patterned misreadings to catch, and to avoid (Richards)

Richards documented how readers reliably fail: **stock response** (importing a ready-made reaction instead of reading what's there), **sentimentality** (emotion in excess of the occasion), and **doctrinal adhesion** (judging by whether the content flatters one's beliefs). A good critique catches these in the text; a good critic avoids committing them in the critique.
