# Feedback Craft

Reference for the fiction-criticism skill. Read when shaping the tone and structure of a critique, especially an editorial letter.

A diagnosis the author can't receive is wasted. This is the discipline that makes criticism usable, and the source of the rubric's autonomy dimensions (#4, #5) and its second gate (no overreach). It matters most in editorial-letter mode, where you're serving the author's book, not writing your own.

---

## The believing game, then the doubting game (Elbow)

Two passes, in order:

1. **Believing.** Grant the work its own logic. Find what it's doing, what's working, and why a smart author made these choices. Steelman before striking. This isn't politeness; it prevents the most common critical error, attacking a book for not being the book you'd have written.
2. **Doubting.** Then press for what fails: the contradictions, the unearned moments, the places the machinery shows. The truer something seems, the harder to doubt it.

A critique that only doubts is a hit piece; one that only believes is a blurb.

## Two kinds of feedback, kept distinct (Elbow)

- **Reader-based:** your actual experience as it happened. "Here's where I leaned in, here's where I got lost, here's where I stopped believing her." Honest, located, first-person.
- **Criterion-based:** judgment against craft - structure, focalization, earned emotion, voice control.

Keep them labeled. "I got bored here" (reader-based) and "the scene is structurally inert because nothing changes in it" (criterion-based) are different claims with different weight; conflating them muddies both.

---

## Gate the fix-its (Lerman's Critical Response Process)

Lerman's process exists precisely to stop responders from rewriting the maker's work. Adapted for a single critic:

1. **Lead with meaning.** Say what landed, what was evocative or strong, before any problem. Not flattery - orientation: the author needs to know what's working so they don't cut it.
2. **Separate neutral observation from opinion.** "The last three scenes are in summary" is an observation. "That drains the ending" is an opinion. Mark the line.
3. **Name the opinion behind a suggestion, then offer a direction, not a draft.** Instead of handing over a rewritten sentence, name the underlying problem and point a direction the author can walk in their own voice. Make the diagnosis so clear the author can find their own fix; people gain more from a path to their own solution.
4. **Ask permission for the hard stuff.** When a note is large or destabilizing, frame it as available rather than imposed: surface the problem and let the author decide whether to go through it.

This is the working meaning of gate G2: never supply the prose. The moment you write the replacement line, you've stopped diagnosing and started co-writing in your voice. If the author then asks for a rewrite, that's a different job (the storytelling skill), and it's theirs to ask for.

---

## Don't homogenize

The writing workshop's documented failure is pressure toward a single house style, every story sanded toward the same approved shape. Inherit the rigor, not the conformity. Work on each text's own terms: a maximalist book and a minimalist one succeed and fail by different standards, and pushing both toward one aesthetic is the wrong-standard error (rubric #2, and the list of what sinks a critique).

---

## Register

- **Direct, not cruel; warm, not sycophantic.** The author should finish knowing exactly where they stand and still wanting to work.
- **Vivid handles** make notes stick: a memorable name for a pattern lets the author see it across the whole manuscript ("you're leaving fingerprints like a melodramatic burglar"). Earn them; don't force them.
- **Proportion praise honestly.** Calibrate to the work's real quality. Hollow praise corrodes trust in everything else you say.
- **Match the mode.** Adversarial reads can hit harder; editorial letters lean further toward the believing pass and gated suggestion. In every mode, evidence is what makes a hard note land instead of wound.
- **Mind the audience.** Notes given in front of others carry social weight that the same notes in private don't. That's a reason to be careful with framing, not to soften the diagnosis.
